The first time Milo noticed it, he laughed.
He was waiting at the crosswalk outside the clinic, one hand wrapped around a paper cup that had gone cold, the other digging for his keys. The pedestrian signal blinked red. The traffic light turned green. A bus hissed past, and in its window he saw his own reflection—tired eyes, unshaved jaw, a stranger’s face wearing his. Nothing remarkable.
Then a woman in a gray coat, walking the opposite direction, glanced at him and said, very quietly, “You should have called her back.”
Milo froze.
The woman did not slow. She did not look again. She slid into the crowd as if she had never existed.
He told himself it was coincidence. That she’d mistaken him for someone else. That the sentence was common enough. People failed each other all the time. They failed phone calls. They failed apologies.
He stepped off the curb anyway, the signal still red, because his feet had already decided for him.
A horn blared. The sound hit him like a slap. A sedan skimmed close enough that he felt its heat. Milo lurched backward, heart clawing at his ribs.
On the sedan’s rear window, someone had scratched a symbol into the dust—interlocking circles and thin lines, precise as a diagram, like a thought made visible. The same symbol was printed on the clinic’s door behind him. The same symbol was stamped, faintly, on the corner of the cup in his hand.
Three times in one minute.
He dropped the cup. It hit the sidewalk and did not spill. It simply sat there, upright, as if gravity had forgotten the instruction.
The world held its breath.
Milo backed away from the cup. Backed away from the door. Backed away from the symbol. The city around him kept moving—shoes, voices, impatience—but a thin hum began to rise under it all, an electrical vibration that didn’t belong to any machine.
His phone buzzed.
A notification, not from any app he recognized, slid onto the screen.
ANOMALY WARNING
Pattern drift detected.
Milo stared until his eyes watered. “That’s not real,” he whispered. “That’s not a thing.”
The hum deepened.
His fingernails hurt.
He looked down and saw bruising bloom along his knuckles in a pattern of circles and lines, as if something had pressed a stamp into his flesh from the inside. The bruises spread with patient speed, mapping his bones like an annotation.
He tried to breathe and couldn’t. His lungs took shallow, panicked bites of air. His heart skipped—an honest misfire—and his vision sparked at the edges.
Around him, the crowd began to thin.
Not because people were leaving. Because people were no longer there.
A man mid‑stride vanished as if edited out of the moment. A teenager’s laugh cut off in the middle and never completed. A bicyclist’s wheel kept spinning for a second after the rider disappeared, then toppled over with an empty clatter.
Milo’s throat tightened until he thought he might vomit. He tried to scream, but the sound came out small, trapped behind a mouth that didn’t feel like his.
He was no longer sure what was happening around him and what was happening inside him.
The clinic door opened.
Two figures stepped out. Their robes were dark and plain, the fabric matte like an absence. Their faces were uncovered, ordinary, calm—the kind of calm that does not belong to humans in an emergency. Each wore a thin cord around the wrist, and on the cord hung a small metal tag stamped with the symbol.
The taller one approached Milo without urgency.
“You’re drifting,” the figure said, voice low, almost kind. “Hold still.”
Milo stumbled backward. “Get away from me. Call an ambulance. Call—call anyone.”
The figure tilted its head. “Ambulances don’t help this.”
“What is this?” Milo demanded. “What did you do to me?”
“We did not do it,” the figure replied. “You did. You listened. You noticed. You became relevant.”
The second figure—shorter, with a scar cutting across the eyebrow—raised a handheld device that looked too simple to be medical. It had no brand, no interface, only a dull lens and a faint pulse of light.
Milo tried to run.
His legs locked—not as if a hand grabbed them, but as if the rhythm of his nerves had been rewritten mid‑step. His knees refused. His calves seized. Panic surged, and the hum surged with it, tightening the world like a belt around his chest.
The scarred figure clicked the device once.
Milo’s vision collapsed into darkness like a curtain being drawn.
As he fell, he heard the taller figure speak again, softly, reverently, the way some people spoke in places of worship.
“The Philosophers will want this one,” it said. “This one can feel the corridor.”
