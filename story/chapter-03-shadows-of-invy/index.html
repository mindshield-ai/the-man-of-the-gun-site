Chapter 3: Shadows of Invy

After the spear, the world did not return to normal. It became louder. Clayton moved through New Elysium with the spear strapped across his back, feeling its presence like a second spine. The etched symbol along the blade did not pulse like a device. It simply existed—quiet, certain, indifferent. He couldn’t decide whether it was protection or surveillance. The city looked the same as it always had—people on sidewalks, trains arriving on schedule, advertisements selling the illusion of choice—but Clayton sensed the thinness beneath it. The hum lived everywhere now, threaded through the air like a vibration in a taut wire. Coincidences stacked up in neat, impossible rows. A man dropped a wallet at Clayton’s feet, and inside was a photograph of a dining table set for eight. A radio in a passing car played a melody that matched a tune Clayton had not heard since childhood. A digital billboard glitched and displayed, for a single breath, the words: YOU ARE BEING MEASURED. Clayton stopped walking and stared at the billboard. When he blinked, it was selling luxury real estate again. By the end of the month, he stopped calling them coincidences. He stopped calling himself paranoid. The pattern was too consistent. Too personal. Late one night, he cut through a grid of backstreets on his way home, avoiding the main avenues where cameras watched more openly. Rain fell in thin sheets, turning the pavement slick and black. Neon signs reflected in puddles like bruises. A warm desert wind carried grit that stuck to wet skin. Clayton sensed the presence behind him before he heard footsteps. The hairs on his neck rose. His breathing tightened. He turned down an alley that should have been empty. A streetlamp there flickered—once, twice—then steadied. Its pale light pooled on wet concrete. “Running from yourself?” a voice called out, amused. Clayton spun, hand dropping to his revolver. A figure stepped out from shadow and into the light. It looked like him. Taller, thinner, as if hunger had shaped the bones. The same eyes, but empty of hesitation. A jagged scar cut from the temple down to the jaw, as if someone had tried to carve a new face into his. The doppelgänger smiled with a mouth that did not belong to a human being. “I’m what you keep avoiding,” it said. “The part of you that learned the lesson.” Clayton’s pulse hammered. “Who are you?” The thing shrugged as if the question was childish. “The better draft.” Clayton raised the revolver. “Stay back.” The doppelgänger laughed—hollow, delighted. “You think you chose this alley?” It lunged with startling speed. Clayton fired. The shot was clean. The bullet tore through the doppelgänger’s shoulder. Blood sprayed and steamed in the cold air. The figure barely flinched. It closed the distance and struck Clayton in the jaw hard enough to crack his teeth together. Clayton hit the ground, vision flaring. He tasted blood. He scrambled backward, barely avoiding a stomp that would have split his skull. The doppelgänger moved erratically, fueled by something that wasn’t adrenaline. It was rage without focus. Invy without object. Clayton’s mind raced. This wasn’t a man. This was something born through him—through what he refused to carry. The revolver’s cylinder clicked once, then stopped. For a half‑second Clayton stared at it in disbelief. Metal should not forget how to be metal. But the hum had thickened, and the air felt charged, as if the alley had become a throat singing one note too loud. The doppelgänger grabbed a rusted pipe and swung. Clayton rolled, the pipe cracking the concrete where his head had been. He yanked the cylinder, trying to force it. It resisted, stubborn as a jaw clenching shut. Four in. Hold. Six out. Seamus’s cadence steadied his hands. The pressure in his skull eased a fraction. The cylinder loosened with a grudging clack. The doppelgänger lunged again. Clayton abandoned the revolver and unslung the spear. The blade moved through the wet air with a clean, brutal certainty. He caught the next swing on the shaft, felt the shock travel up his arms, then drove the spear’s butt into the doppelgänger’s throat. Cartilage cracked. The thing gagged, eyes bright with hate. Clayton twisted and drove the blade through its chest. The doppelgänger stiffened, mouth opening as if to speak—then went still. Clayton expected relief. Instead, nausea rolled through him. The body on the ground was flesh and blood. Real. Human in all the ways that mattered. Whose flesh? Whose blood? A low rumble vibrated through the concrete. The walls around the alley shivered. Windows warped as if viewed through thick water. The city’s geometry softened, bending under pressure. From the distorted space emerged more figures. More versions. Some stretched too long. Some hunched too low. Faces twisted into grotesque exaggerations of Clayton’s features, as if the layer was mocking the concept of identity. They formed a loose circle around him and began to chant in unison, voices merging into a discordant harmony. “Join us. Join us. Join us.” Clayton’s heart pounded. Bullets would not solve this. The world itself was becoming the enemy. He tightened his grip on the spear and let his attention slide toward the hum—not opening the door wide, not surrendering, just peering through the crack. Threads. Not literal strings, but lines of tension in the air, faint alignments connecting each imitation to the space around it. The alley had seams. Clayton moved. He impaled the first clone through the chest. Something dark spilled—too thick, too wrong—and the figure disintegrated into ash. Another lunged with claws extended, and Clayton decapitated it in a clean arc. Each time the spear struck, the air seemed to snap back toward normal, as if the weapon cut the lie at its joints. But the clones kept coming, and the hum climbed until his teeth ached. Then the chant stopped. The clones parted like a crowd making way for a monarch. A figure shuffled forward, hunched, his back distorted into a grotesque hump that made his silhouette wrong. His skin looked stretched thin over bone. His eyes were bright with a fever that did not belong to biology. He carried himself like a man who had been awake too long inside someone else’s dream. Kettleback. Clayton did not know how he knew the name. He simply did. Kettleback’s mouth pulled into something like a smile. “Finally,” he rasped. “The anchor wakes.” Clayton kept the spear raised. “What are you?” Kettleback’s gaze flicked to the spear with something like reverence and resentment. “A patch,” he said. “A compromise.” Clayton frowned. “You’re with the True Believers?” Kettleback coughed a laugh that sounded like rust. “Everyone wants to own the corridor,” he said. “I keep it from collapsing.” The clones around them trembled, as if restrained. The air thickened. Clayton felt pressure behind his eyes. The hum swelled—not an appetite, not a mind, just a force that grew when attention fed it. Kettleback lifted one clawed hand and reality warped. The alley stretched, then folded. Buildings leaned inward. The streetlamp’s light turned syrupy, slow, as if time had become viscous. Clayton staggered, fighting vertigo. He sensed something probing at the edges of his mind—not reading thoughts, but pressing emotion, testing for cracks. Memories rose unbidden. Childhood isolation. The taste of cold coffee. The weight of signing agreements that caged futures. Loneliness like a constant dull ache. Invy fed on those wounds. “Stop,” Clayton snarled, forcing himself forward. “Get out of my life.” Kettleback’s eyes glittered. “Your life is a door,” he whispered. “And doors are meant to open.” Clayton lunged. The spear met Kettleback’s flesh with resistance, like cutting through thick cloth soaked in oil. The blade sank deep. Kettleback screamed, and the sound wasn’t only sound. It rippled through the alley’s seams, making the clones flinch like dogs hearing a whistle. Kettleback convulsed, blood and dark seep mixing on the spear’s blade. “Don’t,” he choked. “If you break me—” Clayton hesitated. In Kettleback’s voice, he heard genuine fear. Not fear of death. Fear of consequence. “What are you holding back?” Clayton demanded. Kettleback’s mouth twisted into something almost human. “Drift,” he whispered. “The sickness. The cascade. The Philosophers call it ‘stabilization.’ The True Believers call it ‘healing.’ The body calls it pain.” Kettleback’s claws rose, aiming for Clayton’s throat. Instinct took over. Clayton drove the spear deeper. Kettleback’s eyes widened. His body shuddered. Then his form began to come apart—not like magic, not like smoke, but like a structure losing the tension that held it together. Light tore away in fragments, as if something inside him had been stretched too far and finally snapped. When the last fragment vanished, the alley snapped back into place—but something had changed. The hum did not fade. It deepened. A fissure ran along the concrete beneath Clayton’s feet, thin at first, then widening, emitting a faint glow like a wound opening. Heat breathed up from it—sterile, surgical heat, like an operating room lamp. Clayton stepped back, pulse racing. “What did I do?” The remaining clones did not attack. They simply looked at him with expressions that might have been pity if they had been human. Then they dissipated into smoke. Clayton stood alone in the alley with the spear dripping. He knew, with cold certainty, that he had not won anything. He had removed a restraint. The city began to unravel. In the hours that followed, New Elysium’s streets filled with chaos that had no single source. Buildings trembled. Windows warped. People screamed at shadows that weren’t there. The air itself seemed to stutter, as if reality was skipping frames. Clayton found Seamus near a shattered transit hub, where the crowd had gathered in panicked clusters. Sand and ash mixed on the wind, sticking to sweating faces. Seamus’s face was drawn tight. “You killed him,” he said without greeting. Clayton’s throat tightened. “He attacked me.” “Aye,” Seamus said, voice raw. “And he was holding something back while he did it.” Clayton looked out at the crowd. A man stumbled past with eyes glazed. His skin was marred with bruises in geometric patterns. His fingers twitched, not like puppet strings, but like nerves out of rhythm. A woman fell to her knees, clutching her side, vomiting dark blood that looked too thick. “These are innocent people,” Clayton said, horrified. “Innocent,” Seamus replied, “and becoming exploitable.” A scream tore through the air. A group of civilians surged toward them, faces contorted, movements wrong—jerky, synchronized, like bodies entrained to the same beat. Their eyes were unfocused, but their bodies moved with purpose. Seamus drew a pair of ornate daggers from beneath his coat. Their blades were etched with the symbol. “They’re riding the wave,” Seamus said. “The Invy finds cracks in panic. And the True Believers will use anything that moves.” Clayton raised his revolver and the spear. The revolver fired twice—then the cylinder stalled again, metal refusing its own function as the hum thickened. Clayton swore, dropped to spear, and fought through bodies that should not have been enemies. He shoved, struck, pinned with the spear’s weight, and only fired when the pressure eased enough for the gun to obey. Every impact felt like a failure. In the midst of the turmoil, figures in dark robes appeared—not panicked, not confused. Calm as the two at Milo’s clinic. Their eyes reflected the hum instead of the world. They murmured in Arabic, Mandarin, Spanish, and tongues Clayton couldn’t place; different sounds, same intent. The meaning arrived in his chest like pressure: be still. submit. be useful. True Believers. One stepped forward, young, sharp‑featured, smiling as if enjoying an argument. “Clayton Harrow,” he called out. “The pattern that thinks it can alter necessity.” Clayton’s jaw clenched. “You caused this.” The Believer spread his hands as if offering a blessing. “You disrupted containment by removing a patch,” he said. “We are here to restore alignment.” “By breaking people?” Clayton snarled. “By preventing collapse,” the Believer replied smoothly. “You think freedom is the absence of constraint. We know better. We guide the currents so your species doesn’t drown in them.” Seamus spat. “You don’t guide. You harvest.” The Believer’s smile thinned. “The Dining Philosophers have taken interest in Mr. Harrow,” he said. “Submit to stewardship. We can keep your body from failing. We can quiet the hum.” Clayton felt a flicker of fear at the mention of body failure. He remembered the bruises on the stumbling man. He remembered Milo’s collapse—forums calling it drift sickness. He remembered the clinical phrase with no explanation. He raised the spear instead of answering. The Believer sighed, disappointed. “Then you leave us no choice.” He gestured. Pressure slammed toward Clayton and Seamus—not wind but force, the air compressing like a fist. Concrete erupted. Glass shattered. The crowd screamed. Clayton braced and forced his breath into Seamus’s cadence. Four in. Hold. Six out. The pressure became survivable. Not safe—survivable. The fight was brutal. Bullets struck something invisible and turned aside. The Believers moved with choreography that wasn’t trained but tuned, their bodies responding to the hum as if it were music. Clayton drove the spear into one at close range. The blade sank. The Believer’s eyes widened, surprised by pain, as if his doctrine had promised him immunity. Blood sprayed. The Believer fell, mouth whispering a prayer that sounded more like a formula than faith. At last, the remaining True Believers retreated, melting into the chaos as if the city itself swallowed them. Seamus stumbled, clutching his side. A deep gash seeped dark red. Clayton caught him and dragged him into the shell of a half‑collapsed building. He tore cloth from his shirt and pressed it to the wound. Seamus hissed in pain. “You’re loud,” he rasped. Clayton’s hands froze. “What?” Seamus’s gaze didn’t waver. “Every time you push against it—every time you open yourself to the hum—it answers back,” he said. “It remembers your shape.” Clayton stared at the spear leaning against the wall, the symbol dark in the gloom. Outside, the city groaned as reality adjusted to a new wound. Clayton swallowed hard. “Then we find the ones doing this.” Seamus gave a rough exhale that might have been laughter. “Aye,” he said. “But don’t waste your blood on robes. Cut one hand and another reaches for the knife.” He coughed, pain tightening his face. “If you want the pressure to ease, you go for the ones who never get their hands dirty.” Clayton said it quietly, tasting the words. “The Dining Philosophers.” Seamus nodded once, then winced. “And the deeper you go, the less the world will pretend to be a world.” Clayton stood, weapon in hand. He looked out at New Elysium—at a city being rewritten while most of its people didn’t even know they were pages. He did not feel hope. He felt resolve, sharp and ugly and necessary. “Then we go deeper,” he said.
